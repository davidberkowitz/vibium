the side elevation and the plan, as built

The resultant, in three dimensions drag to orbit

The side elevation carries x and z; the plan carries x and y. Between them they cover all three axes but never at once, and in a combined maneuver — braking while turning — the force into the driver has all three components simultaneously. So the same vector is drawn three times here from one origin at one scale: whole, then with the lateral axis dropped (all the side elevation can hold), then with the vertical dropped (all the plan can hold). The dashed arrows are visibly shorter and point somewhere else; the angle on each is how far the force leans out of that drawing's plane. That is what the ⊗ in the side view has been admitting since M1, measured. There is no body in this view: a figure was built, and cut, because at every camera angle it read as a zigzag rather than a driver and drew the eye away from the one thing the view is for.

Set what a driver does and everything follows from one solve. The side elevation carries what a profile can show honestly; the plan view carries lateral load, which in profile would only point into the page. Each contact's number is its share of the total, picked by minimum stored elastic energy subject to what that contact can physically do — foam cannot pull, webbing cannot push, and a driver can only brace so hard. Ask for more grip than the surface has and both drawings keep showing the clamped state the tyres can actually deliver, and say so rather than extrapolating.

With body lag on, the occupant follows the cabin through a first-order delay, so mid-maneuver the two drawings deliberately disagree: the tyre loads in plan have already moved while the body in elevation is still arriving. That gap is the readout marked body vs cabin. It closes to nothing whenever the inputs hold still, so every settled number is the same with the lag on or off.

Vibration a different quantity, in different units

Everything above is force in newtons at one instant. This is whole-body vibration: a weighted RMS acceleration in m/s² spread over frequency, evaluated per ISO 2631-1. The two are deliberately not combined — a comfort index averaged into a force arrow would satisfy neither. They share the speed and the road, and nothing else. The weighted trace is what the seated body is sensitive to; the gap between it and the raw trace is the human weighting doing its work, discarding energy the seat really does deliver but the body barely registers.
